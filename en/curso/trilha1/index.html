🤖 Agent, not chatbot

The difference that changes everything: a chatbot tells you how to do something; an agent does it. You give it a goal, it has tools, and it executes.

What is:

A chatbot explains HOW to book a flight; an agent actually books it. The difference is the ability to act in the world.

Why learn:

It's the core concept of all of Track 1. Without it, you use Hermes like just another chatbot and waste its power.

Key concepts:

Talks vs. does; advice vs. execution; response vs. completed action.

What is:

Chatbot = a smart friend who gives tips. Agent = a personal assistant who does the task for you.

Why learn:

The analogy makes the practical difference clearer than any technical definition.

Key concepts:

Advice vs. delegation; you stay in command, it handles execution.

What is:

"Find the cheapest flight from Dubai to Toronto in the next 2 weeks" → it searches, picks one, and opens the result in a nice-looking HTML page.

Why learn:

Shows the goal → research → decision → delivery cycle that defines an agent.

Key concepts:

One goal; multiple autonomous steps; ready-to-use result.

What is:

The agent has access to tools (Gmail, calendar, search, browser), and it acts through them rather than just chatting.

Why learn:

Tools are what turn text into real action—the essence of an agent.

Key concepts:

No tools = chatbot; with tools = agent.

What is:

Hermes is an AI with tools, accessible wherever you are through your phone.

Why learn:

Mobile availability is what makes the agent useful day to day, not just at your desk.

Key concepts:

Mobile access; an agent always at hand.

What is:

For a quick question ("what does X mean?"), a chatbot is enough; an agent shines when there's a TASK to carry out.

Why learn:

Avoids using (and paying for) a heavy tool when a simple answer will do.

Key concepts:

Question = chatbot; task with steps = agent.

🐕 What Hermes is (and when to use it)

Where Hermes fits vs. Claude Code, OpenClaw, and IDEs. It’s your Labrador: it lives with you, knows you, and gets better year after year.

What is:

Hermes is like your dog: it lives with you, knows you, and gets better year after year.

Why learn:

Defines Hermes’s “design ethos”: persistence and growth over time.

Key concepts:

Persistence; long-term relationship; continuous improvement.

What is:

Claude Code is the contractor: excellent for a specific job, but it doesn't live with you or remind you later.

Why learn:

Knowing the difference prevents you from expecting a session tool to have persistent memory.

Key concepts:

Session vs. persistence; precision tool vs. companion.

What is:

OpenClaw is your roommate; anti-gravity is your IDE/coding partner. Each has a different role.

Why learn:

Mapping the ecosystem helps you choose the right tool for each moment.

Key concepts:

Distinct roles; neither replaces the other.

What is:

The essence of Hermes: the more you use it, the more it learns about you and the more useful it becomes.

Why learn:

Sets the right expectation: the value grows over time, not on day one.

Key concepts:

Accumulation effect; value compounds with use.

What is:

Use Hermes when you're on the go — at a coffee shop, at the gym, on your phone. Use desktop tools when you're at your desk.

Why learn:

Knowing the right context for each tool maximizes productivity.

Key concepts:

Mobile = Hermes; desktop = desktop tools.

What is:

Hermes’s design principle is to live with you and improve over time—not be a disposable tool.

Why learn:

This ethos explains decisions like local memory, soul, and backups.

Key concepts:

Persistent companion; growth; relationship.

🧠 One Brain, 22 Mouths

The same intelligence, accessible through 22+ interfaces. Where Hermes runs is the brain; the channels are just mouths plugged into it.

What is:

Where Hermes runs (your PC or a VPS) is the brain. It's the SAME intelligence, no matter how you talk to it.

Why learn:

Understanding that the brain is unique explains why memory and context are shared across channels.

Key concepts:

A central brain; pluggable interfaces.

What is:

Telegram, Discord, WhatsApp, Slack, Matrix, browser, your own OS — 22+ interfaces for the same mind.

Why learn:

You talk to the agent through the channel you already use, without learning a new app.

Key concepts:

Multichannel; no required app.

What is:

Imagine a middle layer: it receives calls from any channel and routes everything to your agent.

Why learn:

The analogy explains how messages from different sources reach the same brain.

Key concepts:

Central routing; channel convergence.

What is:

Since intelligence is central, you’re not tied to a single software or interface.

Why learn:

Reduces vendor lock-in and gives you channel flexibility based on context.

Key concepts:

No lock-in; switch channels freely.

What is:

Since there’s one brain, you can start a conversation on Telegram and continue in the browser without losing the thread.

Why learn:

It's the most immediate practical benefit of having a central brain.

Key concepts:

Continuity across channels; unified memory.

What is:

Besides chat apps, Hermes has its own OS/dashboard as an interface — another mouth for the same brain.

Why learn:

Introduces the concept of an Operating System, explored in more depth in Track 3.

Key concepts:

OS as a channel; unified view.

🏠 Where Hermes Lives

Terminal, local, or VPS: the three possible homes for Hermes, with the cost, control, and security trade-offs of each.

What is:

A terminal is where you enter commands; local means your own computer; a VPS is a computer owned by another company that you rent.

Why learn:

Without this vocabulary, the rest of the module (and the installation) won't make sense.

Key concepts:

Terminal; local; VPS.

What is:

Running it on your PC is free, easy, and secure; it works as long as the computer is on. Many people use an old MacBook 24/7.

Why learn:

It’s the recommended way to get started—zero hosting costs and complete control.

Key concepts:

Free; must be powered on; dedicated machine 24/7.

What is:

You install it by pasting the command into the terminal (Cmd+Space → "terminal" → paste) or asking Claude Code to install it for you.

Why learn:

It's the practical step that takes Hermes off the page and gets it running.

Key concepts:

Command pasted; Claude Code as the installer.

What is:

A VPS runs on another company's computer (hosting): you rent it, pay monthly, and need to protect the ports to prevent attacks.

Why learn:

It’s the option for keeping Hermes running 24/7 without leaving your personal PC on.

Key concepts:

Monthly rent; uptime; attack surface.

What is:

VPS providers often pay referral fees through affiliate links. Not every recommendation is neutral.

Why learn:

Makes you more discerning when choosing a provider and helps avoid biased decisions.

Key concepts:

Affiliate links; commercial bias.

What is:

Since it runs on your machine, you can ask Hermes to "shut down the gateway" or "restart"—it controls its own infrastructure.

Why learn:

Shows the power of local control: the agent manages itself.

Key concepts:

Self-administration; control through natural language.

🔑 OAuth vs. API key

The two ways to connect a model to Hermes. OAuth is the button you can revoke; the API key is the key you keep and can rotate.

What is:

With OAuth, the browser opens, you log in and click “Allow.” The connection is ready, with no need to handle keys.

Why learn:

It’s the simplest and safest way to connect when the provider offers it.

Key concepts:

Login + Allow; no exposed key; revocable.

What is:

An API key is a string of characters stored on a server that grants access to the provider’s models.

Why learn:

It’s the method used when OAuth isn’t available (e.g., Claude) and provides broad access.

Key concepts:

Secret string; access to all of the provider's models.

What is:

You can rotate the API key at any time; once rotated, the old key will never work again.

Why learn:

It's your defense if a key leaks — just rotate it.

Key concepts:

Rotation; immediately revoke the old key.

What is:

Grok and ChatGPT connect via OAuth; Claude does NOT offer OAuth—it’s API key only.

Why learn:

Knowing who offers what prevents you from looking for an OAuth button that doesn't exist.

Key concepts:

OAuth: Grok, ChatGPT; API key: Claude and others.

What is:

You run homes setup in the terminal, choose the provider and then complete OAuth (reauthenticate) or paste the API key.

Why learn:

It's the exact point where the two methods meet in practice.

Key concepts:

homes setup; choose provider; OAuth or paste key.

What is:

OAuth is the button you can "take back" at any time; an API key is a key that needs to be stored securely and can be rotated.

Why learn:

The image illustrates the difference in responsibility between the two methods.

Key concepts:

Revoke vs. keep; convenience vs. control.

🎛️ Choosing the Model

Hermes is an agentic framework; the brain is a swappable model. Multi-brain strategy: the best model for each task.

What is:

Hermes is the agentic framework; the "brain" is a pluggable model. The command /model switches this brain.

Why learn:

Separating the framework from the model is what enables a model-agnostic strategy.

Key concepts:

Fixed framework; swappable model; /model.

What is:

For heavy reasoning, use Opus 4.7/4.8 via OpenRouter, with a spending cap (e.g., US$10/month, then stop).

Why learn:

Expensive model only where it’s worthwhile, with a limit to keep costs from getting out of hand.

Key concepts:

Opus for reasoning; spending cap.

What is:

For high-volume general tasks, use GPT (your ChatGPT via OAuth makes use of the US$20 subscription) or Grok (with X connected, Twitter search).

Why learn:

Makes use of subscriptions you already pay for and reduces cost per token.

Key concepts:

GPT via OAuth; Grok + X; low-cost volume.

What is:

DeepSeek and free models run at almost no cost. For example: DeepSeek V4 flash delivers ~95% of the performance for ~1% of the cost.

Why learn:

For autopilot and background tasks, a cheap model is the obvious choice.

Key concepts:

~95% of the performance, ~1% of the cost; free for high-volume use.

What is:

OpenRouter is one connection that gives you access to hundreds of models, with performance and cost rankings to compare.

Why learn:

Centralizes access and makes it easy to switch models based on the task.

Key concepts:

Single hub; rankings; cost comparison.

What is:

Using a single model for everything is like having only one hammer: everything becomes a nail. The idea is to vary the tool by task.

Why learn:

It's the practical summary of the multi-brain strategy.

Key concepts:

Model-agnostic; the right tool for each job.

💻 Local & Private

Run the MODEL on your machine, not just Hermes. 100% private, works offline — 1000 ft underground, in flight, or in space.

What is:

Local-hosted here means that not only Hermes, but the AI MODEL itself, runs on your machine.

Why learn:

It's what guarantees total privacy and offline operation.

Key concepts:

Local model; no external server.

What is:

Large models have billions of parameters and need a data center. Locally, you’re limited by your hardware.

Why learn:

Sets expectations: privacy costs performance/speed.

Key concepts:

Parameters vs. hardware; lower performance.

What is:

Go to Apple → About This Mac, send Hermes a screenshot, and ask “what’s the most powerful local model I can run?”

Why learn:

It's the practical way to find out what your hardware can handle without guessing.

Key concepts:

Hardware specs; recommendation based on a screenshot.

What is:

You download models via Ollama — e.g., Gemma, Qwen 32B/3.6, or free Cloud options.

Why learn:

It’s the go-to tool for running models locally with just a few commands.

Key concepts:

Ollama; Gemma; Qwen.

What is:

Since everything runs locally, it’s 100% private and works offline: 1000 ft underground, flying, or in space.

Why learn:

It's the main argument for local: nothing leaves your machine.

Key concepts:

Complete privacy; offline; no network dependency.

What is:

Local is a good choice when privacy or offline operation is nonnegotiable; for maximum power, cloud models still win.

Why learn:

Helps you choose between privacy and performance depending on the situation.

Key concepts:

Priority-based decision; privacy vs. power.
